## 1. People side of AI (core)
Most AI rollouts don't fail on the tech.

They fail on 1 question nobody says out loud:
"If the AI gets it wrong, whose name is on it?"

Last month I watched a team go quiet on exactly that. The tool worked. The pilot worked. Then a manager asked who signs off when it sends the wrong quote, and nobody answered.

So people quietly went back to doing it by hand. By hand, at least the blame is clear.

→ Adoption isn't a training problem first. It's an ownership problem.
→ Every AI workflow needs 1 named person who owns the output.
→ "I checked it" should be a normal step, not a sign you don't trust the tool.

People use what they feel safe using.

Who owns the output of AI at your company right now?

## 2. Wellness / yoga (carousel with Sanjana's photos)
I come from a line of yoga teachers, and 1 word from that world keeps saving me in this one: aparigraha. Non-grasping.

Every week there's a new AI tool, a new model, a new "you're falling behind" post. The pull is to grab all of it.

What I actually do:
→ Phone stays out of the bedroom. The first 20 minutes of my day are breath and movement, not the inbox.
→ AI writes the first draft so I can teach my class.
→ 1 new tool a month, max. If it doesn't earn its place in 2 weeks, it goes.

You don't need every tool. You need a clear head when you choose 1.

What's your rule for not drowning in it?

## 3. Technical depth (sprinkled, human)
Sat with a few ops leaders this month about why their AI pilots stall. Same 4 things every time:

→ The agent got access to every tool "just in case". More access means more wasted tokens and less reliable answers. Give it the 3 tools the job needs.
→ They put a copilot on top of messy data. Two CRMs, duplicates everywhere. The agent just finds the mess faster. You need 1 clean layer it reads from first.
→ The SOP describes the ideal path, not the real one. Watch someone do the task before you automate it.
→ Nobody measured a baseline. So 3 months later nobody can say what got faster.

None of these are model problems.

Which one are you stuck on?

## 4. Young founder
A mentor said something to me last Tuesday that I haven't stopped thinking about:

"You don't have a sales problem. You have a follow-up problem you're calling a sales problem."

Ouch. And true.

I came from tech sales. I know how to open a conversation. But as a founder, the follow-up is where things died: the second email, the check-in after the call, the "just circling back" I felt awkward sending.

So we built it into how the company runs. Every follow-up is drafted for us, and a person approves it before it goes.

The lesson wasn't "work harder". It was: find the step you avoid, and make it impossible to skip.

What's the step you keep avoiding?
